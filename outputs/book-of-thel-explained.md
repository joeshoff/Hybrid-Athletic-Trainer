# The Book of Thel (William Blake, 1789): What It Says and What It Means

## Bottom line

Thel is a young, unborn (or pre-experience) spirit who's afraid her life is pointless because it's brief. She asks 4 humble things (a Lily, a Cloud, a Worm, a Clod of Clay) how they can bear being temporary. Each answers the same way: you live for others, you're consumed, and that consumption is the meaning. The Clay invites Thel to see the underworld of mortal life for herself. She goes, reaches her own grave, hears a voice cataloguing what bodies, senses and sex do to a person, shrieks, and runs home.

That's the plot, and the text supports it line by line. What the ending *means* is the one thing the poem refuses to settle, and 200+ years of critics have split on it. Older readers (Damon, Bloom, Frye) mostly read Thel's flight as failure: fear of life. Many later readers argue the reverse: the Lily, Cloud and Clay are selling self-sacrifice, and Thel is right to refuse the deal. I'll lay out both below, with sources, and flag where I couldn't verify something.

## 1. The object itself (facts, not interpretation)

- Dated 1789, probably composed 1788 to 1790. 8 plates in Blake's "illuminated printing" (text and image etched together and hand-colored). 16 copies of the original 1789 to 1793 printings are known. ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/The_Book_of_Thel))
- Written in 14-syllable lines ("fourteeners"), mostly unrhymed. It's the first Blake poem in that meter, which became his main line afterward. ([Literary Encyclopedia](https://www.litencyc.com/php/sworks.php?rec=true&UID=1409); [Britannica](https://www.britannica.com/topic/The-Book-of-Thel))
- The Motto plate was added after the first plates were done; it's an afterthought, and appears to date from about 1790. ([Blake Archive](https://blakearchive.org/copy/thel.b?descId=thel.b.illbk.04); [G. E. Bentley Jr., *Blake Quarterly* 19.4](https://bq.blakearchive.org/19.4.bentley))
- It's the gentle twin of *Tiriel*, an unpublished, much harsher manuscript poem Blake wrote around the same time. A few lines moved from *Tiriel* into *Thel*. ([EBSCO Research Starters](https://www.ebsco.com/research-starters/history/book-thel-william-blake/))

**A note on the text you pasted.** It's a mix of editions. Sections I and part of II keep Blake's own spelling and punctuation ("Lilly," "ceasd & smild," "Mne Seraphim"). The rest is a regularized modern text ("Lily," quotation marks). Nothing is missing, but "Mne Seraphim" is the one spot where editions really differ: it's in Blake's printings, and many reprints change it to "the." ([Wikipedia Reference Desk archive citing the Norton edition](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Wikipedia:Reference_desk/Archives/Language/2014_May_21))

## 2. The names

| Name | What the sources say |
|---|---|
| **Thel** | From a Greek word meaning "desire" or "will." ([Literary Encyclopedia](https://www.litencyc.com/php/sworks.php?rec=true&UID=1409); [Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/The_Book_of_Thel)) |
| **Mne Seraphim** | The Norton edition notes that Cornelius Agrippa's occult philosophy names "Bne Seraphim," the sons of the seraphim, as intelligences of the planet Venus. The suggestion is that Blake altered it. That's a scholarly proposal, not something Blake stated. ([Norton, via Wikipedia Reference Desk](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Wikipedia:Reference_desk/Archives/Language/2014_May_21)) |
| **Vales of Har** | Thel's home, a place of innocence and "the sweetest flowers." *Har* is Hebrew for "mountain," so "vales of Har" is an ironic phrase (valleys of the mountain). In *Tiriel*, Har is a senile ruler who stands for stale moral law. ([Har (Blake), Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Har_(Blake))) |
| **Luvah** | The Cloud's steeds drink where "Luvah doth renew his horses." One source reads Luvah here as a sun spirit, like Apollo. ([RPO / search summary](https://rpo.library.utoronto.ca/content/book-thel)) |
| **Adona** | I didn't find a source that explains it. I'm not going to guess. |

## 3. The Motto

> Does the Eagle know what is in the pit? / Or wilt thou go ask the Mole: / Can Wisdom be put in a silver rod? / Or Love in a golden bowl?

The second pair of lines rewrites Ecclesiastes 12:6: "Or ever the silver cord be loosed, or the golden bowl be broken," a passage about death and the body falling apart. ([Bible Hub](https://biblehub.com/ecclesiastes/12-6.htm); Michael Ferber, ["A Possible Source for 'Thel's Motto'"](https://bq.blakearchive.org/9.2.ferber) and Tolley & Ferber, ["Likely & Unlikely Sources"](https://bq.blakearchive.org/10.1.tolley), *Blake Quarterly* 9.2 and 10.1)

Milton's *Comus* is the other commonly proposed source. ([Hilda Hollis, "Seeing Thel as Serpent," *Blake Quarterly* 30.3](https://bq.blakearchive.org/30.3.hollis))

Readings that published sources actually offer:

- **Eagle vs. Mole:** the eagle (high, eternal vision) can't know the pit; only the mole, who lives in it, can. So: can you know embodied life without being in it? And can wisdom and love exist inside a physical body at all? ([RPO / search summary](https://rpo.library.utoronto.ca/content/book-thel))
- **Rod and bowl as the two sexes:** the silver rod and golden bowl as male and female bodies, or more bluntly, genitals. ([same](https://rpo.library.utoronto.ca/content/book-thel))
- **Rod as scepter:** a swipe at church and royal authority. This one shows up in summaries with weaker sourcing; treat it as a minority reading.

The plain point: the motto says Thel's question (what is mortal life for?) can't be answered from outside. You have to go down into the pit. Which is exactly what she does, and exactly what she won't finish.

## 4. Walking through it

### Section I: Thel and the Lily

Thel's sisters tend their flocks; she wanders off alone to mourn. Her lament is a list of transient things: rainbow, cloud, reflection, shadow, infant's dream, a smile. Everything fades. She wants to die gently and hear "the voice / Of him that walketh in the garden in the evening time" (the voice of God in Genesis 3:8, before the Fall).

The Lily answers: I'm tiny and weak, but God visits me every morning. I'll melt in summer's heat and "flourish in eternal vales." So why complain?

Thel's reply matters. She doesn't argue with the Lily's theology; she points out the Lily is *useful*. It feeds the lamb, perfumes the grass, tames the steed. Thel isn't useful. "I vanish from my pearly throne, and who shall find my place?" The Lily sends her to the Cloud.

### Section II: The Cloud

The Cloud says vanishing isn't ending: "when I pass away, / It is to tenfold life, to love, to peace, and raptures holy." He descends as rain, marries the dew, and together they feed the flowers.

Thel's answer sharpens her problem. She smells flowers and hears birds but feeds neither. Everyone will say: "Without a use this shining woman liv'd, / Or did she only live to be at death the food of worms?"

The Cloud jumps on that: then being worm food *is* a use. "Every thing that lives / Lives not alone, nor for itself." He calls the Worm.

### Section III: The Worm and the Clod of Clay

The Worm can't speak; it just weeps, like a naked infant. The Clod of Clay, a mother, bends over it and "her life exhal'd / In milky fondness." Clay says she's cold, dark and the lowest thing there is, but God pours oil on her head, marries her, calls her "mother of my children," and gives her "a crown that none can take away." Then the key admission: "how this is, sweet maid, I know not, and I cannot know; / I ponder, and I cannot ponder; yet I live and love."

Thel is moved. She knew God loved the worm; she didn't know he *nourished* it. The Clay invites her in: "'tis given thee to enter / And to return."

That promise ("and to return") is what makes the ending possible.

### Section IV: The pit

A porter lifts "the northern bar." Thel sees the dead, the roots of every heart twisting into the ground, "a land of sorrows & of tears where never smile was seen." She reaches her own grave plot and hears a voice asking 9 questions. Read them in order:

1. Why can't the Ear shut out its own destruction?
2. Why can't the Eye shut out the poison of a smile?
3. Why are eyelids loaded with arrows, hiding armed men?
4. Or an Eye that showers gifts and gold?
5. Why a Tongue coated with honey from every wind?
6. Why an Ear that sucks everything in like a whirlpool?
7. Why a Nostril breathing in terror?
8. Why a "tender curb upon the youthful burning boy?"
9. Why "a little curtain of flesh on the bed of our desire?"

The list moves through the senses (ear, eye, tongue, nose) and ends on touch and sex. Line 8 is restraint on male desire; line 9 is almost certainly the hymen. Critics read the voice as describing embodied life as a trap: the senses are open doors that let in seduction, violence and fear, and sexuality comes with shame and restriction attached. ([summary of the Allen Ginsberg Project lecture notes and McCollister](https://allenginsberg.org/2018/06/w-j-13-william-blake-the-book-of-thel-4/))

Thel shrieks, runs back "unhinder'd" to Har. End of text.

The final plate isn't text: a girl (maybe Thel) and 2 smaller children ride a serpent, and she holds the reins. ([EBSCO](https://www.ebsco.com/research-starters/history/book-thel-william-blake/); [Hollis](https://bq.blakearchive.org/30.3.hollis))

## 5. So what does the ending mean? (Where critics actually split)

The poem doesn't say whether running was right. Here's who argues what.

### Reading A: Thel fails. She flees life.

- **S. Foster Damon, Harold Bloom, Northrop Frye** (the big mid-20th-century Blake critics) tend to read Thel as passive, fearful and self-absorbed. Her flight is a refusal to be born into experience. ([summary via Łuczyńska-Hołdys, *Blake Quarterly* 46.4](https://bq.blakearchive.org/46.4.luczynska))
- **Frye**, in *Fearful Symmetry* (1947): "Thel is an imaginative seed: she could be any form of embryonic life from a human baby to an artist's inspiration, and her tragedy could be anything from a miscarriage to a lost vision." ([Fearful Symmetry PDF, McMaster Frye blog](https://macblog.mcmaster.ca/fryeblog/wp-content/uploads/sites/4/2010/04/FS.pdf))
- The common framing: an unborn spirit visits the world of generation, rejects its self-sacrifice, and returns to eternity. Innocence that won't risk Experience stays sterile.

In this reading, the Lily, Cloud and Clay are right, and Thel lacks the nerve.

### Reading B: Thel sees through it. She refuses a bad deal.

- **Deborah McCollister, "The Seduction of Self-Abnegation in *The Book of Thel*"** (*Blake Quarterly* 30.3, 1996/97): the Clay *seduces* Thel toward self-sacrifice. What Thel sees at her grave is her real destiny on earth: eternally dying, mournful, useful as mate and mother, and alone. Seeing it lets her reject it. ([BQ 30.3](https://bq.blakearchive.org/30.3.mccollister))
- **Małgorzata Łuczyńska-Hołdys, "'Life exhal'd in milky fondness': Becoming a Mother in *The Book of Thel*"** (*Blake Quarterly* 46.4): the three interlocutors' message shouldn't be read as something Blake wanted Thel to accept. What scares Thel off is a philosophy of utility and self-denial, and her refusal reads as independence, not cowardice. ([BQ 46.4](https://bq.blakearchive.org/46.4.luczynska))

Notice what this reading does with the text. Every one of Thel's guides is female or feminized (the Lily is a "virgin," the Cloud courts the dew, the Clay is a "matron" and mother) and every one's value is being consumed by someone else. And the Clay admits she can't explain why her sacrifice is blessed: "I know not, and I cannot know." Reading B takes that admission seriously.

### Other angles with real sources behind them

- **Hilda Hollis, "Seeing Thel as Serpent"** (*BQ* 30.3): reads the images, not just the words. The pictures cast Thel as a false or parody Christ who imitates giving without giving, and the closing serpent confirms her as "Female Will" (the meaning of her name). This is closer to Reading A, but built on the designs. ([BQ 30.3](https://bq.blakearchive.org/30.3.hollis))
- **Robert Carr, "Divine Construct and the Individual Will: Swedenborgian Theology in *The Book of Thel*"** (*Colby Quarterly* 23.2, 1987): Swedenborg's theology (Blake was involved with the Swedenborgian New Church around then) explains the Cloud's cycle of dying into new life, and places both salvation and damnation inside Thel's choice. ([Colby Digital Commons](https://digitalcommons.colby.edu/cq/vol23/iss2/5/))

### My read, and where it's supported

I think the poem is built to stay open, and the text supports that more than it supports either verdict:

- The Clay promises Thel she can return. So flight isn't a transgression inside the poem's own rules.
- The Motto asks whether you can know the pit without being the mole. Thel goes down, learns enough to be horrified, and leaves: she gets the knowledge and declines the life. Whether that's wisdom or failure is exactly the question the Motto asks and doesn't answer.
- The final image (Thel riding the serpent, in control) doesn't fit "she ran away in terror" cleanly. Critics read it both ways.

That's a judgment, not a citation. The 2 critical camps above are the sourced part.
